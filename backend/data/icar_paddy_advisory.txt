1. SOWING AND NURSERY MANAGEMENT
Use certified, disease-free seeds. Treat seeds with recommended fungicide before sowing to prevent
seed-borne diseases. Maintain a thin film of water in the nursery bed. Nursery duration is typically
20-25 days before transplanting.

2. TRANSPLANTING
Transplant healthy 20-25 day old seedlings at 20x15 cm spacing, 2-3 seedlings per hill. Avoid
transplanting during peak heat hours; early morning or late evening is preferred.

3. WATER MANAGEMENT
Maintain 2-3 cm standing water during the vegetative stage. Drain the field briefly during active
tillering to encourage root development, then reflood. Water stress during panicle initiation and
flowering stages causes the most significant yield loss - these are the most water-critical periods.

4. FERTILIZER MANAGEMENT
Apply fertilizer in split doses: basal dose at transplanting, first top dressing at active tillering
(around 30 days after transplanting), and second top dressing at panicle initiation (around 55-60 days).
Sandy soils leach nutrients faster and benefit from smaller, more frequent doses. Clay soils retain
nutrients longer and are more prone to nitrogen loss through waterlogging if over-irrigated.

5. PEST AND DISEASE MANAGEMENT
Common paddy diseases include:
- Blast (Pyricularia oryzae): appears as spindle-shaped lesions on leaves; favored by high humidity
  and excess nitrogen. Avoid excess nitrogen application; use resistant varieties where possible.
- Bacterial Leaf Blight: yellowing and drying of leaf margins; spreads rapidly in warm, humid, windy
  conditions; avoid field-to-field water flow during outbreaks.
- Sheath Rot: affects the flag leaf sheath, reducing grain fill; more common in dense, over-fertilized
  crops with poor aeration.
- Brown Spot: oval brown lesions on leaves, often linked to nutrient-deficient soils.
Regular field monitoring during tillering through flowering stages is critical for early detection.

6. HARVESTING
Harvest when 80-85% of grains have turned golden yellow, typically 30-35 days after flowering.
Delayed harvest increases grain shattering losses. Drain field water approximately 10 days before
the expected harvest date to allow the field to dry for easier harvesting.

7. POST-HARVEST AND MARKET TIMING
Distress selling immediately after harvest, when local supply is high, typically yields lower prices.
Farmers with on-farm storage capacity can benefit from monitoring mandi price trends across nearby
markets and holding produce briefly if prices are expected to improve, weighed against storage costs
and quality risk from moisture/pests during storage.
